Learn Personal Branding with Phoebe · Session 2 of 6

Your niche: the onliness test

Most profile headlines could be pasted onto anyone else's profile and stay just as true. That is the problem. A stranger who finds you has a few seconds to work out who you help and why it should be you, and a line built from shared vocabulary gives them nothing to hold. Tonight you write the opposite: one line about your work that the person at the next desk could not sign, small enough to be true, specific enough to be checked, and backed by something a stranger can open.

Part 1 · covers positioning for one person

A line nobody else could sign

6 min live

Positioning sounds like something companies pay agencies for. For one person it is narrower and cheaper: a sentence that tells a stranger which problem you are the person for. The test of that sentence is not whether it sounds impressive. It is whether it would still be true with someone else's name on it.

LiveThe onliness statement, adapted for one person3 min▶

Marty Neumeier's book Zag (2006) is reported to argue that a brand should be able to say it is the only one that does a particular thing, and to offer a minimal template for saying it. As reported, the template runs: Our [offering] is the only [category] that [benefit]. The exact wording in the book was not verified for this page, so treat that as a reported summary of the idea, not his sentence.

For one person, the template needs three swaps. The offering is you. The category is the field you work in, said the way the people who hire you would say it. The benefit is what changes for a specific group of people, which is where most drafts go vague. A working version for an individual:

I help [audience] [change] by [method]. Proof: [artefact].

Every slot is a noun or a number somebody could check. "Businesses" is not an audience; "grocery planners" is. "Drive growth" is not a change; "cut stockouts" is. A method is something you actually do. A proof is something a stranger can open. If a slot can only be filled with an adjective, it is not filled yet.

Self-studyThe competitor-swap test3 min▶

Take your line, cross out your name, and write in the name of someone who does roughly what you do: a colleague, a peer at another company, the person who last got the role you wanted. Read it again. If it is still true for them, it does not yet distinguish you; a stranger could not use it to choose you over them.

This is a practitioner test, widely used in positioning work, and it is not attributed to Neumeier here. It is useful because it is fast and because it is uncomfortable in the right way: it shows how much of a line is borrowed vocabulary. It has one blind spot. A line can pass the swap and still be wrong for you, because it is false, or because it names a niche you do not want. Passing proves the line is yours; it does not prove the line is good.

Company positioning, naming and visual identity follow the same instinct at a larger scale, and they have their own home: learn-ai-branding-with-phoebe.

Real world

Ines's Draft 1 (constructed) read "Passionate, results-driven data enthusiast helping businesses unlock insights and drive growth." Put any other analyst's name on it and it stays true; thousands of analysts could sign it tomorrow. It is not a bad sentence. It is just not about her, and a stranger who reads it learns nothing they would not assume from the job title.

Part 2 · covers how small a niche can be

The arithmetic of a small niche

5 min live

The fear behind vague lines is that a specific one shrinks the audience. It does, on purpose. The best-known argument that a small audience can be enough is Kevin Kelly's essay "1,000 True Fans", and it is worth reading at source because it is more careful, and more openly uncertain, than the way it is usually quoted.

LiveKelly's arithmetic, both versions3 min▶

Kevin Kelly's page on The Technium is dated March 4, 2008, and it carries two versions of the argument. At the top sits a shorter rewrite (it appeared in Tim Ferriss's Tools of Titans; the page does not give the year). Below it is the 2008 original. They do not use the same number in the same way, and quoting one as the other is the most common mistake made with this essay.

  • The rewrite. A true fan is someone who "will buy anything you produce". Each makes you $100 of profit a year, so 1,000 fans make $100,000 a year.
  • The 2008 original. Each true fan spends $100 a year on your work, framed as one day's wages. That is spending, not profit.

Kelly then says plainly that "The number 1,000 is not absolute": at $50 a fan you need 2,000, at $200 you need 500. And the arithmetic only works under one condition, a direct relationship: "they must pay you directly". He also writes, about his own model, "My formula may be off by an order of magnitude". Read together, the essay is a sketch of how small a direct audience can be, offered with its uncertainty on the label.

Self-studyThe case against, published by Kelly himself2 min▶

On April 27, 2008, Kelly posted "The Case Against 1000 True Fans" on the same site. In it Jaron Lanier challenges the idea from experience: he says he has not found a single musician who meets the definition. Kelly published the challenge next to his own argument rather than answering it away.

That is the honest state of the evidence. The 1,000 is a model, its author flagged how far off it could be, and a serious critic disputed whether anyone fits it, on the author's own page. Nobody has settled it, and nothing here settles it either.

The transfer, as an interpretation. Most people building a professional reputation are salaried, not selling to fans, so the money arithmetic does not carry over. What carries over is the smallness. For a salaried professional the niche is the small set of people who hire you, refer you and cite your work: often dozens, not thousands. A line written for them can afford to be narrow, because they are the only readers who change anything. That reading is ours, not Kelly's. Segments, funnels and channels as a business discipline belong to learn-marketing-with-phoebe.

Real world

A common hiring situation. A team needs someone who has forecast perishable demand before. The hiring manager asks two colleagues and searches a handful of names. The people in that loop, the manager, the two colleagues and the recruiter, are the whole audience that matters for that role, and a line that speaks to grocery planners speaks to all of them. A line written for "businesses" speaks to none of them in particular.

Part 3 · covers measuring the line

Count what the line names

6 min live

The swap test is a judgement. This part adds a count, so you can see where a line is spending its words. The checker below is a heuristic: it matches words against four fixed lists and prints how many fall in each. It does not read meaning, and it does not grade you. It shows how much of a line names who it is for, what changes for them and what proves it, against how much is vocabulary every profile shares.

LiveReading the four counts3 min▶

Press each draft button, then type your own line. The checker prints four counts and a percentage, each marked heuristic.

  • Audience words: who the line is for, from a list of roles and sectors (planners, clinics, warehouses, founders).
  • Outcome words: what changes for them (cut, fewer, catch, stockouts, delays).
  • Proof: any number, or a word for something a stranger can open (teardown, repo, talk, case study).
  • Vague words: the shared vocabulary (passionate, results-driven, insights, thought leader).

Ines's three constructed drafts score as follows. Draft 1: 11 words, audience 0, outcome 0, proof 0, vague 7, specificity 0 percent. Draft 2, "Data analyst who builds forecasting dashboards for retail teams.": 9 words, audience 1, outcome 0, proof 0, vague 0, specificity 100 percent. Draft 3, "I help grocery planners cut stockouts with forecasts they can check, one public teardown at a time.": 17 words, audience 2, outcome 4, proof 1, vague 0, specificity 100 percent. Two test lines are worth trying too: "Seasoned, strategic thought leader and lifelong learner." scores 7 words and vague 4, and "Helping 3 warehouse teams find late orders before 9am." scores 9 words, audience 1, outcome 2, proof 2.

A good line is not one with a high percentage. It is one with no zero in the first three counts and nothing in the fourth.

Self-studyWhat the heuristic cannot see3 min▶

Draft 2 is the warning. It scores 100 percent specificity, the same as Draft 3, with exactly one audience word. That word is "analyst", and it names Ines, not the people she helps. The checker cannot tell the difference between the writer and the reader; it only knows the word is on a list. Read the counts, not the percentage: outcome 0 and proof 0 say Draft 2 is still missing two of the three things a stranger needs.

The lists are finite. A word that is not on a list scores nothing. "Retail" and "teams" are not on the audience list, so Draft 2 gets no credit for them, and a line in a field the lists do not know, a sharp line about veterinary rostering, say, can score low while being exactly right. All four lists are printed under the widget so you can see what it is looking for.

It cannot check truth. In the test line, "3 warehouse teams" counts as proof because it contains a number, and "teardown" in Draft 3 counts because it is a listed word. Whether the three teams or the teardown exist is for a stranger to check, which is the reason to link them. Ines's first teardown does not exist yet; session 3 builds it, which is why her line promises a habit rather than claiming a count. A count of zero is a useful prompt; a high count is not a verdict.

Real world

A headline review between a manager and a report often stalls on taste: "it sounds a bit flat". Running the line through four counts turns the conversation into nouns. "Outcome zero: what changes for the people you help?" is a question someone can answer at the desk, and the answer usually becomes the line.

Build-along

Three drafts to one line

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps, one loop, on your own line. Ines went round the loop three times; most people need two or three passes. Work in a plain document under your session 1 audit, and keep every draft: the drafts you cut are the evidence of what you decided not to be.

Lay out the three sentences other people used about your work. From reviews, introductions, thank-you emails: last week's homework. Underline every noun that names a person, a problem or a thing you made. Those nouns are better raw material than anything you would write about yourself, because someone else chose them.

★ Step 1 · borrowed wordssentence 1: "..." nouns: ______ sentence 2: "..." nouns: ______ sentence 3: "..." nouns: ______ Ines (constructed): "her forecast caught the stockout before the buyers did" nouns: forecast, stockout, buyers

Fill the template. One noun or number per slot. If a slot will only take an adjective, leave it blank for now; a blank is more honest than "innovative", and it tells you what to go and find.

★ Step 2 · the templateI help [audience] [change] by [method]. Proof: [artefact]. audience: who, named the way they name themselves change: what is different for them afterwards method: what you actually do artefact: something a stranger can open

Run the competitor swap. Write the name of a real peer in place of yours and read the line aloud. If it is still true for them, find the slot that made it true and make it narrower: a smaller audience, a more specific change, a proof only you have.

★ Step 3 · the swapmy line, with ______'s name on it: still true for them? yes / no which slot made it true? audience / change / method / proof narrower version of that slot: ______________________

Paste it into the checker above and read the four counts. Write them down next to the draft. Ignore the percentage. Look for a zero in the first three and anything above zero in the fourth; and if your audience count is 1, check whether the word names your reader or you.

★ Step 4 · four countsdraft __: audience _ outcome _ proof _ vague _ Ines, Draft 2 (constructed): 1 0 0 0 ("analyst" is her) Ines, Draft 3 (constructed): 2 4 1 0

Cut every vague word and replace it with a noun or a number you can show. Not a synonym: "passionate" becomes nothing, "drive growth" becomes the specific thing that grew, with the count of times you have done it. Then go round the loop again from step 3.

★ Step 5 · cut and replacecut: ______ -> replaced with: ______ cut: ______ -> replaced with: ______ Ines: "unlock insights" -> "cut stockouts" "drive growth" -> "one public teardown at a time"

Write the final line where a stranger will meet it, and date it. Put it in your profile headline today, and add it to the session 1 audit sheet with the date. Session 3 turns the proof slot into a public artefact, so if your proof is still a blank, that is fine: it is the next thing you build.

★ Step 6 · ship the linefinal line: ______________________________________ date: YYYY-MM-DD placed in: profile headline [ ] audit sheet [ ] counts: audience _ outcome _ proof _ vague _

Keep the losing drafts. Paste Draft 1 and Draft 2 under the final line with their counts. In session 6 you will review the line against a fresh search audit, and the drafts show what you chose to leave out and why.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Run the swap with a real person. Show your line to a colleague with their own name written over yours and ask: is this true of you? A "yes" tells you which slot to narrow.
  2. Ask one person in your niche to read it cold. Someone who hires, refers or cites people like you. Ask them what they think you do. If their answer is not your line in their words, redraft.
  3. List the proof you already have. Every artefact a stranger could open: a notebook, a write-up, a talk, a dashboard you are allowed to show. Mark the one that best backs the line.
  4. Carry the line everywhere it appears. Profile headline, conference bio, email signature, the about page if you have one. Same words on each, so a stranger who finds two of them sees one person.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · You put a peer's name on your positioning line and it is still true for them. What has the competitor-swap test detected?

The swap test checks whether a line is yours, not whether it is true or good. A line true for everyone around you tells a stranger nothing about you in particular. Passing the swap is necessary, not sufficient.

2 · Someone quotes Kelly: "you need exactly 1,000 fans each paying $100 profit". What does Kelly's own page actually say?

Kelly writes that "The number 1,000 is not absolute", ties it to a direct relationship, and says his formula "may be off by an order of magnitude". The two framings on his page, profit and spending, are different claims and should not be merged.

3 · Ines's Draft 2 scores 100 percent specificity in the checker, the same as Draft 3. Why is that misleading?

With no vague words, any single listed word gives 100 percent. The checker is a word-list heuristic: it cannot tell a word about the writer from a word about the reader. Read the four counts, not the percentage.
